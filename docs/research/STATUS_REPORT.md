# Equity Factor Book: Status Report

The most consequential number this project produced is 365.10 basis points per year. That is how much more a backtest earns if it buys today's index members and assumes they were always in the index, compared with a universe that was genuinely investable at the time. Only 44.8 percent of the members the index ever deleted still have recoverable price history in the free data used here, so a survivor-only panel does not merely miss a handful of names: it systematically deletes the companies that failed. The number grew rather than shrank as the data was cleaned, because correcting reused ticker symbols and share counts removed noise that had been partly cancelling the bias.

That single measurement is a fair description of what the repository is for. It contains no signal with a demonstrated edge, no strategy that should be traded with real money, and no claim that one is waiting to be found. What it contains is every component of a systematic equity risk and portfolio management stack, built from scratch on free data, each component measured against a numeric threshold written down before the number was seen, and each documented so that the same machinery can be moved to corporate credit. A reader who wants the machinery, the failures and the reasons for each choice will find them here. A reader who wants a return series should look elsewhere.

## What this project is and why it exists

The Equity Factor Book (EFB) builds every component of a systematic equity risk and portfolio management stack from scratch, using the two books by Giuseppe Paleologo as the syllabus: *Advanced Portfolio Management* and *The Elements of Quantitative Investing*. The goal is not a tradeable signal but the ability to build and defend each piece of the machinery, on free data, in a form that ports to corporate credit. The roadmap puts it plainly: build the machinery, not an edge. No signal here is claimed to have an edge, a profitable strategy is not required for the project to succeed, and a negative result counts as a complete outcome if it prevents a bad decision later.

Free data is a deliberate constraint: prices and share counts from a retail vendor, index membership from a pinned Wikipedia revision of the S&P 500 changes table, factor returns from the Kenneth French library, sectors from the Wikipedia constituents table, and a daily SPY holdings file for the ongoing constituent source. That forces the project to confront missing history, revised history, reused ticker symbols and non-point-in-time sector maps, which is where the interesting engineering lives.

Equities before credit, for three reasons. Equity prices are liquid and daily, so a factor model is testable on a clean panel before the same mathematics meets instruments that trade by appointment. Equity cross-sections are wide, five hundred names a day, which makes a daily cross-sectional regression well posed. And equities supply a large free panel of failures, the delisted and the acquired, which is what makes survivorship bias measurable rather than theoretical. Credit is the destination, but its data problems deserve to be met by someone who has met the easier version.

## The plan

The work is thirteen sprints in three tiers. Tier one, E1 to E3, is the data and model foundation: data, time-series models, the cross-sectional risk model. Tier two, E4 to E8, is the risk model and construction stack: covariance, model evaluation, hedging, signals and construction. Tier three, E9 to E13, covers costs and capacity, dynamic risk allocation, the paper-traded book, attribution, and the credit port design. The first eleven sprints are built and on the main line. E12, the performance attribution engine, is built on its own branch and its criteria cannot be evaluated until the live book has thirty trading days behind it. E13, the credit port design note, is written and complete, because it is a statement about the repository rather than about the book.

Every sprint follows the same loop: a concept is stated, given an intuition, written in its academic formulation, implemented, tested empirically, interpreted the way a portfolio manager would use it, and written up as a research artifact. A concept that stops before the last two steps is a textbook exercise. Each sprint opens with three research questions, one academic, one practitioner and one research, and ends with a document for a reader who has not seen the code.

Every sprint also writes down, before the numbers exist, what would falsify it. A pre-registered falsification criterion is a numeric threshold stated in advance, stored in the sprint's plan, and evaluated afterwards without being reworded. Writing the threshold first stops a researcher who sees a disappointing number from inventing a reason the number was the right one. Pre-registration turns that discretion into a recorded verdict, and no criterion is ever narrowed after the fact.

Three research gates sit in front of the major engineering efforts, each a checklist answered with stored numbers and each with a documented exit for a negative answer. Their answers now exist. RG-Data at the end of E1 passed with its exit clause exercised: the probe coverage and the survivorship measurement failed their thresholds, the universe was adapted (36 reused ticker symbols excluded and recorded), and E2 started on the adapted data. The milestone gates G1 (E1 to E3 end to end) and G3 (the construction stack running end to end under the champion model with the E8 constraint set) both passed. RG-Signal at the end of E7 returned all six signals NULL, so construction ran on synthetic alpha with a known information coefficient, and the book runs as a documented null book. RG-Operate before E11 is not cleared: its one negative item, the ongoing constituent source, is stood up but not yet applied, so the live book still prices a frozen universe.

## What has actually been built

**Sprint E1, the data layer.** The academic question was what a return is and which definition suits which operation, the practitioner question was whether the prices, universe and risk-free rate can be trusted, and the research question was how large the survivorship bias is. The sprint built a price and returns pipeline with explicit missing-data, delisting, staleness and outlier policies; a point-in-time universe rebuilt by walking the index changes table backwards; a performance library with Sharpe standard errors; dashboard tab D0 for data health; and an append-only ledger recording every data decision with its reason. Four of five criteria failed, each with a stored number. Coverage of current index members was complete, but coverage of all requested tickers was 77.16 percent against a 95 percent bar, the returns file has 302 interior missing return days across 13 tickers, and the adjusted-close audit averaged 0.018 basis points but peaked at 221.25 basis points on one merger day. The equal-weight universe return correlates with the French market return at 0.9564. The survivorship measurement failed its threshold in the most informative way: 44.8 percent of deleted members have recoverable price history against a 70 percent bar, the naive backtest that buys all current members earns 365.10 basis points per year more than a point-in-time universe, and that universe earns 8.24 basis points per year more than the French market. The rule a portfolio manager takes from E1: every long-only result here carries a survivorship caveat.

**Sprint E2, time-series models and volatility.** The questions were how a stock's factor exposure is estimated and how uncertain that estimate is, and whether shrunk or adaptive estimators beat raw ones out of sample. The sprint built a model version registered as TS-v1: market and factor betas with classical and Newey-West standard errors, Vasicek and Blume shrinkage, rolling and exponentially weighted betas, several volatility estimators including GARCH, Fama-French five-factor plus momentum residuals, and a portfolio risk history for two seed books, on dashboard tab D1. The registry was introduced here: a model version with its parameters and the hash of the data it was fitted on, and a champion rule written before any number was seen. TS-v1 is not champion and not eligible, because the rule requires a champion to be refreshable daily from the project's own data. Nine of thirteen criteria passed, among them full-sample betas agreeing with rolling ones at 0.9244. Four failed, three of them in the volatility horse race: GARCH beat trailing volatility for 58.2 percent of the 98 names it could fit and the decay 0.94 estimator for only 35.4 percent, against a sixty percent bar, and production stayed with a decay 0.97 estimate. What a portfolio manager takes from E2: use a shrunk rolling beta with a Newey-West standard error for hedging, size positions off idiosyncratic volatility rather than total volatility, and treat an eyeballed Sharpe ratio, which here carries a standard error near 0.23, as a hypothesis rather than evidence.

**Sprint E3, the cross-sectional model.** The questions were how cross-sectional returns decompose into systematic and idiosyncratic pieces and whether the premia are distinguishable from zero, how much portfolio risk comes from unintended factor exposures and which position reduces risk fastest if trimmed, and whether the model explains realized risk well enough to size a book. The sprint built a fundamental cross-sectional model registered as XS-v1: seven style descriptors, eleven sector factors, a weighted least squares fit of 17 estimated columns per day with the market factor as the intercept; factor-mimicking portfolios; a factor covariance matrix and a shrunk specific variance; a risk decomposition for both seed books; Fama-MacBeth premia with Newey-West t statistics; and dashboard tab D2. XS-v1 runs from January 2011 over 3,941 sessions. Eight of nine criteria passed. The average cross-sectional R squared is 0.3295. XS-v1's identity for the factor-mimicking portfolios holds to 1.2 times ten to the minus thirteen. Its momentum factor correlates with the French momentum factor at 0.756. The E3 tercile diagnostic located the momentum book's error in the factor covariance, not the specific variance, and E4 owns the fix.

**Sprint E4, covariance and the residual-factor audit.** The question was which covariance estimator prices risk out of sample, and whether a common factor is missing from XS-v1. The sprint raced nine estimators (sample, EWMA, Ledoit-Wolf, clip, PCA-v1, PCA-v1c, XS-v1, TS-v1, constant-correlation) over 175 rolling 504-day windows, scored by out-of-sample minimum-variance volatility, and audited the specific returns for residual structure. The largest residual eigenvalue is 22.303 against a Marchenko-Pastur edge of 3.952, with 23 eigenvalues above the edge, so the fundamental model's specific returns still carry common variation that its columns do not span. Five of seven criteria passed. Two PCA variants were registered, and the sprint's fix, a covariance conditioned on the book's own exposure, is the acceptance test for E3's bias failure.

**Sprint E5, model evaluation and the champion.** The sprint evaluated six model versions across four portfolio families with bias statistics and coverage, applied the champion rule written in E2, and declared the champion. No version is calibrated: the champion XS-v1 has mean absolute bias deviation 0.0607 and a stored stress haircut of 1.8471, from the fraction it underforecast in its worst named episode. Three of five criteria passed. The champion is provisional, and the stress haircut travels with it.

**Sprint E6, hedging.** The sprint measured three hedge types: the exact in-model FMP hedge, an ETF minimum-variance hedge on SPY, IWM and QQQ plus eleven sector funds, and a single-factor beta hedge. The exact FMP hedge drives the worst post-hedge exposure to 5.8e-15 and the idio share to 1.0, at the cost of trading 461.9 names on average per rebalance. The ETF hedge removes 0.978494 of factor variance on the long-only book with 13.03 instruments. All seven criteria passed. The hedge policy for the book is the exact FMP hedge, never the capped form.

**Sprint E7, the signal laboratory.** Six signals were built and run through the RG-Signal checklist: idio_momentum, low_residual_volatility, momentum_12_1, post_earnings_drift, short_interest and short_term_reversal. All six returned NULL; the deciding numbers are stored. The best-behaved, momentum_12_1, has a horizon-1 IC of 0.0151 that survives both a forward shift and an extra-day lag, which is what a paper-trading instrument needs. Four of six criteria passed. Construction therefore proceeds on synthetic alpha with a known information coefficient, and the book runs as a documented null book with the expected E12 verdict of luck written down first.

**Sprint E8, construction.** The sprint built position construction on synthetic alpha with a known IC across five seeds, compared seven rules (proportional, sharpe, shrunk, constrained and unconstrained mean-variance, Procedure 6.3, combined), and measured the transfer coefficient against both N and the effective breadth N_eff. The effective breadth is 128.705 against N of 456.63. Six of eight criteria passed. The champion construction is the proportional rule hedged with the exact FMPs, and constrained mean-variance buys robustness at a transfer-coefficient cost.

**Sprint E9, cost and capacity.** The sprint built the transaction-cost model (a size-decile spread schedule from 10 bp to 1 bp, impact k times sigma times sqrt of dollar over ADV with k of 0.5, commission 1 bp one side, borrow 2 percent per year), a turnover-penalized optimizer, and capacity curves. The spread cost of a high-turnover rebalance is about 0.07 percent of AUM, not the 4 percent a raw Corwin-Schultz reading implied; the capacity question is impact. Two criteria passed, two failed and one is recorded not evaluable.

**Sprint E10, risk allocation.** The sprint ran a Kelly analysis (full Kelly 9.781, half Kelly 4.891 against a design-book Sharpe of 0.978 with standard error 0.268), drawdown distributions against analytical benchmarks, a stop-loss and a re-entering stop, and volatility targeting. Half Kelly is chosen. The drawdown stop and volatility targeting do not earn their keep, and the risk budget is written per VIX regime rather than as one number. Three of six criteria passed.

**Sprint E11, the daily paper book.** The sprint assembled the live loop: an evening proposal (alpha, Procedure 6.3 sizing, the exact FMP hedge, E8 constraints, E9 costs, E10 vol scale), a morning paper execution under two fail-safe guards, daily reconciliation, a thirty-day clock, and the dashboard. NAV is 1,000,000 paper, final. dry_run stays true until the owner flips it once. Taking the book live is a story of its own, and `docs/research/E11_live_launch.md` tells it in full: the seed that had to move to object storage because the deployed disk is wiped on every deploy, the four first-contact defects, the construction problem on a small account, a cost estimate that put 37.2460 of its 39.0447 basis points into one stock nobody trades, a hedge that was one session stale, and an alpha that multiplied a variance where its contract says volatility. The construction decision surface is a nine-row table (minimum position 1,500, 2,000, 3,000 and 5,000 dollars applied iteratively to a fixed point, top N by alpha at 150 and 200, one flagged two-part floor and one flagged share-only floor, and the full 499-name reference book), each row re-hedged on its own subset and renormalized to gross 1.0. The row now running is the share-only floor at 20 shares, which keeps 158 names and an effective breadth of 94.2573.

**Sprint E12, attribution.** The sprint builds the machinery that explains every dollar the book makes or loses: a holdings-based decomposition of profit into factor and idiosyncratic pieces, a returns-based regression as a cross-check, and a test for skill against luck built on Sharpe standard errors. Its criteria are windowed on the live book, so they are stored as pending and cannot be evaluated honestly until thirty trading days of real proposals exist. The sprint also corrected the clock: the thirty-day window starts on the day the owner flips the book out of dry run, not on the day the loop first wrote a proposal, because a dry-run proposal is the machinery exercising itself rather than the book trading.

**Sprint E13, the credit port design.** The sprint writes, as a document and with no code, what in this stack transfers to corporate credit and what has to be rebuilt, so that the credit project starts from an inventory rather than from an opinion. It is deliberately designed as a statement about the repository: it walks 90 modules, records 66 as unchanged, 34 as re-specified and none as dropped, and cites 163 numbers back to the 37 stored files they came from. A test recomputes the module counts from the tree and resolves every citation, so the note cannot drift away from the code it describes. Three places where unchanged is the wrong answer for credit have a section of their own below.

Across the thirteen sprints the repository holds the hashed artifacts under a single manifest hash, a test suite that takes about twelve minutes to run in full, and a dashboard with a tab per sprint. The registry holds the model versions, with XS-v1 the provisional champion and its stress haircut stored beside it, and it also carries the parameters the live loop runs on.

## The findings

**Survivorship bias got worse, not better, after the data was cleaned.** The revision moved the wrong way: the first measurement was 349.67 basis points per year, correcting ticker identity took the bias to 365.81, and a second restatement settled it at 365.10, against the 44.8 percent of deleted members whose price history is recoverable. Had that work been skipped, every long-only result would carry a survivorship number about five percent too small.

**A ticker symbol is not an identity.** One implausible day, a ticker whose adjusted close jumped from 0.196 to 18.90, turned out to be a reused symbol, a 2026 listing taking the ticker of a 2010 to 2011 index member. The general check found 36 reused symbols; the contaminated rows sat where a long-short book does its damage.

**The simplest volatility estimator was not beaten, and the project accepted that.** GARCH and a decay 0.94 estimator each failed to beat a trailing 252-day estimate for more than sixty percent of names, on a QLIKE loss. The criterion is written on the out-of-sample window, so it fails and stays failed.

**A book that re-sorts every month has an exposure that static betas cannot see.** The momentum book's static decomposition put its factor share of variance at 0.0968; measuring it with the weights actually held put the factor share at 0.7390. No risk number for a long-short book may be quoted without the measurement method attached.

**The residual-factor audit found a missing common factor.** The largest residual eigenvalue, 22.303 against a Marchenko-Pastur edge of 3.952, says the fundamental model's specific returns still carry common variation the 17 columns do not span. E4 quantified it and handed the survivor-only cross-section to the data layer.

**The sector map is frozen at today's members, which is the binding data defect.** The sector dummies confine the model to the 502 names the sector file holds, a snapshot of current members, so the historical cross-section contains only companies in the index today. In 2010 the restriction removes 41.9 percent of the index by name and 8.85 percent by market capitalization.

**No risk model version is calibrated, and no two families of model are wrong in the same way.** XS-v1 wins the champion rule with mean absolute bias deviation 0.0607, and the stored stress haircut of 1.8471 multiplies a stress forecast. The champion is provisional; the haircut is the honesty about that. The deeper finding is that no family of models is calibrated: a bias statistic of exactly 1.0 would mean the forecast volatility matched realized volatility on average, and no version reaches it, because each family produces its own characteristic error instead. A book calibrated on one family's numbers will be wrong in a different direction when it is sized on another's, which is why the haircut travels with the model name rather than being applied to the book.

**The exact FMP hedge is perfect in-model and the ETF hedge is nearly so for the long-only book.** The exact hedge zeroes modeled exposure to 5.8e-15 at the cost of trading 461.9 names, and for a momentum book the residual size and liquidity exposures survive, which is the cost of hedging with instruments rather than algebra.

**All six signals are NULL, and that is a complete result.** RG-Signal returned NULL for every signal. The construction machinery then ran on synthetic alpha with a known IC, which tests the machinery more cleanly than a real signal would, and the book runs as a documented null book with the expected verdict of luck written down before it started.

**The cost of a high-turnover rebalance is impact, not spread, and the free data is where the estimate is weakest.** The spread schedule says a raw reading of 4 percent overstates the cost by orders of magnitude; the spread cost of a high-turnover rebalance is about 0.07 percent of AUM. Capacity is therefore impact-limited, the lowest-IC book is cost-impaired at any size, and impact is the term a free data set can least support, which is what the one-dollar floor finding below makes concrete.

**Every signal the project built is a known factor, and that is a finding about the signals rather than about the gate.** RG-Signal returned NULL for all six, and the closest to a survivor is the twelve-month momentum signal, whose horizon-1 information coefficient of 0.0151 survives both a forward shift and an extra day of lag. That same signal is price momentum, and the project's own momentum factor correlates with the French library's momentum factor at 0.7555: a free public factor explains most of the raw version of it. A signal that a published factor already spans is not a discovery, and the gate is right to treat the residual, not the raw statistic, as the thing to test.

**On a small book the binding constraint is rank, not risk appetite, and the right measure of breadth is not the name count.** The daily cross-sectional fit estimates 18 columns, and a book held on fewer effective names than that cannot hedge those directions independently, because the names are correlated with one another. The E8 horse race measured this directly: the effective breadth of the seed cross-section is N_eff, 128.705, against 456.63 names, and the transfer coefficient computed over N_eff, about 1.75, is roughly double the same coefficient computed over the raw name count, about 0.93. Breadth, not the number of tickers, is what a long-short book is actually paid for. The construction table built for E11 shows the same geometry in miniature: its tightest rows keep 45 names, and each row is re-hedged on its own subset, which is why those rows report their own residual exposures rather than claim the exact neutrality the 499-name reference book achieves.

**A one-dollar floor turned a missing value into a headline number.** The cost model divides by average daily volume, and for the name in question the corrected series has zero volume on 2,409 of 4,208 sessions, because a missing value had been filled with a floor of one dollar. Impact priced against one dollar of volume is astronomical, so the bug produced a single tidy figure, SW at 37.2460 basis points, which was 95.4 percent of the 39.0447 basis point establishment cost for the whole book. The honest cost for that name is a small fraction of the frozen book's 53.73 basis points, and the general lesson is the one worth keeping: a silent fill inside a ratio leaves no trace in the output, and the result it produces can be read as a fact about the world for as long as nobody divides it back out.

**One position can dominate a book's risk, and the cap that controls it moves the risk rather than deleting it.** The largest single-name specific volatility in the live book belongs to a position whose own price moved 176.97 percent in one session, after which its modeled specific volatility stood at 18.45 percent per day, or 293 percent annualized. Left alone it would have carried more than 40 percent of the book's predicted specific variance, so the live sizing caps any one name's share of that variance at 10 percent. The cap is the right control and worth stating plainly, because a capped book looks diversified on the position report while the risk has merely been redistributed across the remaining names.

**The hedge was one session stale, and the obvious repair could not have fixed it.** The exact hedge is built by algebra: for any design matrix and any book, the hedge that solves the normal equations leaves the product of the design and the hedged book exactly zero, no matter which session the design came from. So the hedge reported clean exposure while it was neutralizing a descriptor row published one session before the book it protected, and the error was invisible in every hedged exposure the project had printed. Measured instead against the row the model actually publishes, the stale hedge leaves 1.2e-15 and 2.9e-14 of exposure on ordinary session pairs and 5.5e-03 and 4.5e-03 across a holiday, and 1.8e-03 on the seed hedge. The repair prefers a stored row dated the session after the book, falls back to building one from data through the book's date with the cross-section taken as the names priced that day, and reports which of the two it used. It was accepted against a 5e-03 bound, with a negative control confirming that the old path fails it.

## The live book

The live book is a paper book: it proposes orders and records them as if executed, and it never sends anything to a broker. Its net asset value is 1,000,000, fixed for the life of the exercise. What runs each evening is a scheduled job on Render that fires at 22:30 UTC, inside the after-hours window, and walks five steps in order. It extends its own data series by fetching the evening's closes and appending them to the stored panel; it refreshes the slow inputs, which are share counts, sector assignments and the daily exchange-traded fund holdings file that the project uses as its ongoing constituent source; it builds the proposal, meaning the alpha, the construction rule, the exact factor-mimicking-portfolio hedge, the constraint set, the cost model and the volatility scale; it executes in paper and reconciles the result against the previous night's stored positions; and it notifies the owner by email, with the run status in the subject line, failing loudly rather than writing a partial day.

Two independent things have to be true before a proposal is priced, and calling both of them a gate is a frequent source of confusion, so they are worth separating. The first is the sanity gate: a check that the loop's own fetched closes are consistent with the stored series before anything is priced, run on two closes the loop fetched on its own evenings through the deployed scheduler rather than through a catch-up or a replay. The second is the staleness rule: the run stops entirely if any input is older than it is allowed to be, counted in trading sessions against the most recent completed session, with the universe allowed one session of slack and every other input allowed none. Staleness is measured per input, because the failure mode for a slow input is not a bad value but a fetch that quietly stops happening.

The flip is a single variable, `dry_run`, and it is the owner's to set. While it is true the loop does everything except place orders, which is how the machinery is rehearsed without touching the market and without accumulating results that would have to be thrown away. The owner flips it once, deliberately, and that day is day 1 of the live clock; the loop then runs on indefinitely with a thirty-trading-day reporting window in front of it. The rule was recorded with its reasoning: before the flip, the deployed dashboard must have rendered a full stored day with every panel populated from real data, because a page that has never displayed a real proposal is not confirmed as working. One earlier attempt at a start was voided for exactly this reason, and the run in place now is a rehearsal that will be superseded when the owner flips the variable.

E12 measures what the book did over that window in three readings: a holdings-based decomposition of profit into factor and idiosyncratic pieces, a regression of the book's returns on the factor returns as a cross-check, and a skill test built on Sharpe standard errors rather than a point estimate. Because the book runs on a documented null signal, the expected verdict is luck, and that expectation is stored before the window opens. If the attribution finds a factor exposure the hedge did not neutralize, that is a machinery defect. If the excess return sits inside its standard error, the signal was null and the machinery did what it was told.

## Three places where unchanged is the wrong answer for credit

The credit design note's central claim is that most of the mathematics transfers unchanged, and it is careful to say where that stops. Three columns and one absence are the exceptions, and each is a rebuild rather than a parameter change.

The market column cannot be reused as it stands. A credit portfolio has no exposure to equities in the sense the equity model means, so a regression whose design carries an equity market factor alongside spread factors will read a spread-hedged portfolio as carrying zero systematic risk, when what it carries is zero credit-market risk and a full position in interest rates. Duration-matched Treasury excess returns enter as the regressand, and the market factor's role changes with it: the column to be estimated is a spread factor, and the rate exposure is handled by matching duration rather than by loading.

The factor covariance matrix cannot be estimated the same way either. A spread duration times spread loading, usually written as the DTS loading, is only interpretable if the covariance of the spread factors is estimated from spread series, and an equity covariance transplanted into a credit book would produce risk numbers with the right shape and the wrong units. The note records this as a rebuild rather than a parameter change, because the input series themselves are different objects.

The third exception is an absence rather than a column: nothing in the cross-sectional model as built reads a value descriptor. Equity value characteristics enter only through what they do to the style columns, and credit's central descriptor is a level rather than a return. A spread level is exactly the kind of input a DTS loading multiplies, and it appears nowhere in XS-v1, so the first credit task with no equity counterpart is a value descriptor that carries a level.

## Blockers and limitations

The cross-section is survivors only, the share count history starts late, the risk-free series ends before the price series, and the constituent source is frozen, all as recorded in the earlier sprints. E4 owns the survivor-only cross-section and the coverage gap, and E5's answer is that no version is calibrated, so every risk number carries a stress haircut. The ongoing constituent source now exists and archives a dated file each evening, but it is not yet applied and it carries no history, so the point-in-time record starts accumulating from the day it began.

The strongest limitation is the one that applies to the most recent work. E13's deliverable is a document, so the credit port has been designed and not tested: every claim in it about what transfers is a claim about the mathematics and about the repository's own inventory, and the first credit sprint is where those claims meet data. The module map and its counts are checked by a test, but no credit factor model has been estimated, no credit covariance has been built, and no credit book has been hedged.

The paper book's own evidence is unsettled by construction: its live window has not opened, its attribution criteria are stored as pending, and the branch carrying them stays unmerged until the window produces numbers. Until then the project's only claims about the book are about its mechanics, meaning what it fetched, what it proposed, what its exposures were, and whether it obeyed its own limits.

The paper book runs on a documented null signal, so E12 will attribute luck rather than skill. That is the design: the machinery is what is being validated, not the signal.

## What the process itself taught

The early sprints taught that documentation is a verification step, not a summary of work already verified, and that a stored probe record catches the zero that arithmetic says is correct. The versioning discipline, the manifest hash and the revisions block, is what makes a correction auditable: the entry recording a correction carries the old value beside the new one, so a reader sees both the error and its size.

The habit that produced the most valuable work in the last two sprints is worth naming, because it runs against the obvious instinct. Every defect found in that period was found by reading the project's own output rather than its code. The stale hedge was invisible in every hedged exposure the repository printed, because the algebra that produces those exposures is correct for any vintage: it was found by asking what the design row was dated, and then measuring the hedge against the row the model actually publishes rather than the one it was built from. The one-dollar cost floor produced a plausible number for a single name, and what exposed it was dividing the result back out and noticing that one name accounted for almost the entire book's cost, which is not something a name can do. The credit note's missing module was found by enumerating the tree and comparing the count with the note's own claim, which is how a document that had passed every check inside it turned out to be four modules short. In each case the code was doing exactly what it said it did, and the only way to see the error was to read what it produced and ask whether the number could be true.

The last rule is that no figure in a walkthrough may be typed by hand. Each sprint's walkthrough notebook reproduces the sprint's numbers from the artifacts, asserts each printed value against its stored counterpart, and ends with a cell that collects every stored value into a forbidden set. The same rule governs this report, which is prose and therefore cannot be executed, so a traceability test enforces it instead: every decimal in these pages has to appear in a stored artifact, with its sign intact, and the test fails the moment a quoted number and a stored measurement disagree. That is why the survivorship bias here is written as the stored value rather than a rounder one, and why a figure taken from an older sprint is quoted as that sprint stored it, even where a later sprint corrected it.

## What comes next and what it is for

The next measurable event is not a sprint. It is the flip, and then thirty trading days of a paper book doing one uninteresting thing every evening. E12 turns that window into a verdict, and the verdict is built so that it can come back as a machinery failure rather than as a signal result, because the book runs on a documented null signal and the point of running it is to watch the machinery behave. Until then, every claim the repository makes about the live book is a claim about mechanics, and it is checked nightly by the run itself.

E13's purpose is different in kind. It is where the project stops proving things about equities and states, with an audit trail, what it would take to move the same mathematics onto corporate credit. Its central claim is about reuse: most of the linear algebra transfers unchanged, and the value of having built the equity version first is that the failure modes are the same and the equity panel is large enough to find them before the data gets hard. What has to be rebuilt is the data and the meaning of the columns, and the note is explicit that those rebuilds are untested until a credit sprint begins.

The project's value is therefore not in any of its numbers taken alone. It is that every component was measured against a threshold fixed in advance, that the negative results were kept and dated, and that the machinery can be handed over with the record of what it does and does not do. That is what the equity version was for, and it is the only thing the credit version will inherit without having to re-derive it.

Numbers here are quoted as the project stores them; where a figure reads as rounded, it is rounded for speech and the stored value is longer. Every decimal in this report is checked against a stored artifact by a test, so the stored value is the authority in every case.
