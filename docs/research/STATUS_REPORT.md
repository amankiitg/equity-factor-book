# Equity Factor Book: Status Report

## What this project is and why it exists

The Equity Factor Book builds every component of a systematic equity risk and portfolio management stack from scratch, using the two books by Giuseppe Paleologo as the syllabus. The goal is not a tradeable signal but the ability to build and defend each piece of the machinery, on free data, in a form that ports to corporate credit. The roadmap puts it plainly: build the machinery, not an edge. No signal here is claimed to have an edge, a profitable strategy is not required for the project to succeed, and a negative result counts as a complete outcome if it prevents a bad decision later.

Free data is a deliberate constraint: prices and share counts from a retail vendor, index membership from a pinned Wikipedia revision of the S&P 500 changes table, factor returns from the Kenneth French library, sectors from the Wikipedia constituents table, and a daily SPY holdings file for the ongoing constituent source. That forces the project to confront missing history, revised history, reused ticker symbols and non-point-in-time sector maps, which is where the interesting engineering lives.

Equities before credit, for three reasons. Equity prices are liquid and daily, so a factor model is testable on a clean panel before the same mathematics meets instruments that trade by appointment. Equity cross-sections are wide, five hundred names a day, which makes a daily cross-sectional regression well posed. And equities supply a large free panel of failures, the delisted and the acquired, which is what makes survivorship bias measurable rather than theoretical. Credit is the destination, but its data problems deserve to be met by someone who has met the easier version.

## The plan

The work is thirteen sprints in three tiers. Tier one, E1 to E3, is the data and model foundation: data, time-series models, the cross-sectional risk model. Tier two, E4 to E8, is the risk model and construction stack: covariance, model evaluation, hedging, signals and construction. Tier three, E9 to E13, covers costs and capacity, dynamic risk allocation, the paper-traded book, attribution, and the credit port design. Through E11 the first eleven sprints are built; the paper book exists as a dry run and waits on the owner to start the clock.

Every sprint follows the same loop: a concept is stated, given an intuition, written in its academic formulation, implemented, tested empirically, interpreted the way a portfolio manager would use it, and written up as a research artifact. A concept that stops before the last two steps is a textbook exercise. Each sprint opens with three research questions, one academic, one practitioner and one research, and ends with a document for a reader who has not seen the code.

Every sprint also writes down, before the numbers exist, what would falsify it. A pre-registered falsification criterion is a numeric threshold stated in advance, stored in the sprint's plan, and evaluated afterwards without being reworded. Writing the threshold first stops a researcher who sees a disappointing number from inventing a reason the number was the right one. Pre-registration turns that discretion into a recorded verdict, and no criterion is ever narrowed after the fact.

Three research gates sit in front of the major engineering efforts, each a checklist answered with stored numbers and each with a documented exit for a negative answer. Their answers now exist. RG-Data at the end of E1 passed with its exit clause exercised: the probe coverage and the survivorship measurement failed their thresholds, the universe was adapted (36 reused ticker symbols excluded and recorded), and E2 started on the adapted data. The milestone gates G1 (E1 to E3 end to end) and G3 (the construction stack running end to end under the champion model with the E8 constraint set) both passed. RG-Signal at the end of E7 returned all six signals NULL, so construction ran on synthetic alpha with a known information coefficient, and the book runs as a documented null book. RG-Operate before E11 is not cleared: its one negative item, the ongoing constituent source, is now stood up but not yet applied.

## What has actually been built

**Sprint E1, the data layer.** The academic question was what a return is and which definition suits which operation, the practitioner question was whether the prices, universe and risk-free rate can be trusted, and the research question was how large the survivorship bias is. The sprint built a price and returns pipeline with explicit missing-data, delisting, staleness and outlier policies; a point-in-time universe rebuilt by walking the index changes table backwards; a performance library with Sharpe standard errors; dashboard tab D0 for data health; and an append-only ledger recording every data decision with its reason. Four of five criteria failed. Coverage of current index members was complete, but coverage of all requested tickers was 77.16 percent and of tickers with ten years of history 71 percent, against a 95 percent bar. The returns file has 302 interior missing return days across 13 tickers, so the zero-NaN criterion failed. The adjusted-close audit averaged 0.018 basis points against a close-plus-dividend series but peaked at 221.25 basis points on one merger day, so the maximum criterion failed. The equal-weight universe return correlates with the French market return at 0.9564. The survivorship measurement failed its threshold in the most informative way: 44.8 percent of deleted members have recoverable price history against a 70 percent bar, the naive backtest that buys all current members earns 365.10 basis points per year more than a point-in-time universe, and that universe earns 8.24 basis points per year more than the French market. The rule a portfolio manager takes from E1: every long-only result here carries a survivorship caveat.

**Sprint E2, time-series models and volatility.** The questions were how a stock's factor exposure is estimated and how uncertain that estimate is, how much a beta of 1.3 should be believed, and whether shrunk or adaptive estimators beat raw ones out of sample. The sprint built a model version registered as TS-v1: market and factor betas with classical and Newey-West standard errors, Vasicek and Blume shrinkage, rolling and exponentially weighted betas, several volatility estimators including GARCH, Fama-French five-factor plus momentum residuals, and a portfolio risk history for two seed books, on dashboard tab D1. The registry was introduced here: a model version with its parameters and the hash of the data it was fitted on, and a champion rule written before any number was seen. TS-v1 is not champion and not eligible, because the rule requires a champion to be refreshable daily from the project's own data. Nine of thirteen criteria passed, among them full-sample betas agreeing with rolling ones at 0.9244, a mean pairwise residual correlation of 0.0156, and an equal-weight book bias statistic averaging 1.0225. Four failed, three in the volatility horse race: GARCH beat trailing volatility for 58.2 percent of the 98 names it could fit and the decay 0.94 estimator for only 35.4 percent, against a sixty percent bar, and production stayed with a decay 0.97 estimate. What a portfolio manager takes from E2: use a shrunk rolling beta with a Newey-West standard error for hedging, size positions off idiosyncratic volatility rather than total volatility, and treat an eyeballed Sharpe ratio, which here carries a standard error near 0.23, as a hypothesis rather than evidence.

**Sprint E3, the cross-sectional model.** The questions were how cross-sectional returns decompose into systematic and idiosyncratic pieces and whether the premia are distinguishable from zero, how much portfolio risk comes from unintended factor exposures and which position reduces risk fastest if trimmed, and whether the model explains realized risk well enough to size a book. The sprint built a fundamental cross-sectional model registered as XS-v1: seven style descriptors, eleven sector factors, a weighted least squares fit of 17 estimated columns per day with the market factor as the intercept; factor-mimicking portfolios; a factor covariance matrix and a shrunk specific variance; a risk decomposition for both seed books; Fama-MacBeth premia with Newey-West t statistics; and dashboard tab D2. XS-v1 runs from January 2011 over 3,941 sessions. Eight of nine criteria passed. The average cross-sectional R squared is 0.3295. XS-v1's identity for the factor-mimicking portfolios holds to 1.2 times ten to the minus thirteen. Its momentum factor correlates with the French momentum factor at 0.756. The E3 tercile diagnostic located the momentum book's error in the factor covariance, not the specific variance, and E4 owns the fix.

**Sprint E4, covariance and the residual-factor audit.** The question was which covariance estimator prices risk out of sample, and whether a common factor is missing from XS-v1. The sprint raced nine estimators (sample, EWMA, Ledoit-Wolf, clip, PCA-v1, PCA-v1c, XS-v1, TS-v1, constant-correlation) over 175 rolling 504-day windows, scored by out-of-sample minimum-variance volatility, and audited the specific returns for residual structure. The largest residual eigenvalue is 22.303 against a Marchenko-Pastur edge of 3.952, with 23 eigenvalues above the edge, so a common factor is missing from the fundamental model. The survivor-only cross-section is quantified: the size factor correlates 0.577780 between the panel and the mapped universe, and the mapped universe is the survivor-only one. Five of seven criteria passed. Two PCA model variants were registered, and the sprint's fix, a covariance conditioned on the book's own exposure, is the acceptance test for E3's bias failure.

**Sprint E5, model evaluation and the champion.** The sprint evaluated six model versions across four portfolio families with bias statistics and coverage, applied the champion rule written in E2, and declared the champion. No version is calibrated: the champion XS-v1 has mean absolute bias deviation 0.0607 and a stored stress haircut of 1.8471, from the fraction it underforecast in its worst named episode. Three of five criteria passed. The champion is provisional, and the stress haircut travels with it.

**Sprint E6, hedging.** The sprint measured three hedge types: the exact in-model FMP hedge, an ETF minimum-variance hedge on SPY, IWM and QQQ plus eleven sector funds, and a single-factor beta hedge. The exact FMP hedge drives the worst post-hedge exposure to 5.8e-15 and the idio share to 1.0, at the cost of trading 461.9 names on average per rebalance. The ETF hedge removes 0.978494 of factor variance on the long-only book with 13.03 instruments. All seven criteria passed. The hedge policy for the book is the exact FMP hedge, never the capped form.

**Sprint E7, the signal laboratory.** Six signals were built and run through the RG-Signal checklist: idio_momentum, low_residual_volatility, momentum_12_1, post_earnings_drift, short_interest and short_term_reversal. All six returned NULL; the deciding numbers are stored. The best-behaved, momentum_12_1, has a horizon-1 IC of 0.0151 that survives both a forward shift and an extra-day lag, which is what a paper-trading instrument needs. Four of six criteria passed. Construction therefore proceeds on synthetic alpha with a known information coefficient, and the book runs as a documented null book with the expected E12 verdict of luck written down first.

**Sprint E8, construction.** The sprint built position construction on synthetic alpha with a known IC across five seeds, compared seven rules (proportional, sharpe, shrunk, constrained and unconstrained mean-variance, Procedure 6.3, combined), and measured the transfer coefficient against both N and the effective breadth N_eff. The effective breadth is 128.705 against N of 456.63. Six of eight criteria passed. The champion construction is the proportional rule hedged with the exact FMPs, and constrained mean-variance buys robustness at a transfer-coefficient cost.

**Sprint E9, cost and capacity.** The sprint built the transaction-cost model (a size-decile spread schedule from 10 bp to 1 bp, impact k times sigma times sqrt of dollar over ADV with k of 0.5, commission 1 bp one side, borrow 2 percent per year), a turnover-penalized optimizer, and capacity curves. The spread cost of a high-turnover rebalance is about 0.07 percent of AUM, not the 4 percent a raw Corwin-Schultz reading implied; the capacity question is impact, and the lowest-information-coefficient book is cost-impaired at any size. Two criteria passed, two failed and one is recorded not evaluable.

**Sprint E10, risk allocation.** The sprint ran a Kelly analysis (full Kelly 9.781, half Kelly 4.891 against a design-book Sharpe of 0.978 with standard error 0.268), drawdown distributions against analytical benchmarks, a stop-loss and a re-entering stop, and volatility targeting. Half Kelly is chosen. The drawdown stop and volatility targeting do not earn their keep, and the risk budget is written per VIX regime rather than as one number. Three of six criteria passed.

**Sprint E11, the daily paper book.** The sprint assembled the live loop: an evening proposal (alpha, Procedure 6.3 sizing, the exact FMP hedge, E8 constraints, E9 costs, E10 vol scale), a morning paper execution under two fail-safe guards, daily reconciliation, a thirty-day clock, and the dashboard. NAV is 1,000,000 paper, final. dry_run stays true until the owner flips it once. The construction decision surface is a seven-row table (minimum position 1,500, 2,000, 3,000 and 5,000 dollars applied iteratively to a fixed point, top N by alpha at 150 and 200, and one flagged two-part floor), each row re-hedged on its own subset and renormalized to gross 1.0, with net dollar, realized beta, share counts and the quantization-error tail on every row. The owner picks from the table; nothing has chosen for the owner.

Across the eleven sprints the repository holds the hashed artifacts under a single manifest hash, a growing test suite, and a dashboard with eleven tabs. The registry holds the model versions, with XS-v1 the provisional champion and its stress haircut stored beside it.

## The findings

**Survivorship bias got worse, not better, after the data was cleaned.** E1 measured that only 44.8 percent of the index's deleted members have recoverable price history, and that the naive backtest of today's members earns 365.10 basis points per year more than a point-in-time universe. The revision moved the wrong way: the first measurement was 349.67 basis points per year, correcting ticker identity took the bias to 365.81, and a second restatement settled it at 365.10. Had that work been skipped, every long-only result would carry a survivorship number about five percent too small.

**A ticker symbol is not an identity.** One implausible day, a ticker whose adjusted close jumped from 0.196 to 18.90, turned out to be a reused symbol, a 2026 listing taking the ticker of a 2010 to 2011 index member. The general check found 36 reused symbols; the contaminated rows sat where a long-short book does its damage.

**The simplest volatility estimator was not beaten, and the project accepted that.** GARCH and a decay 0.94 estimator each failed to beat a trailing 252-day estimate for more than sixty percent of names, on a QLIKE loss. The criterion is written on the out-of-sample window, so it fails and stays failed.

**A book that re-sorts every month has an exposure that static betas cannot see.** The momentum book's static decomposition put its factor share of variance at 0.0968; measuring it with the weights actually held put the factor share at 0.7390. No risk number for a long-short book may be quoted without the measurement method attached.

**The E3 tercile diagnostic located the momentum book's error in the factor covariance.** Across terciles of the book's own momentum exposure the predicted volatility barely moves while realized volatility falls, and the factor term does not respond. The fix, a covariance conditioned on the book's own exposure, is E4's deliverable.

**The residual-factor audit found a missing common factor.** The largest residual eigenvalue, 22.303 against a Marchenko-Pastur edge of 3.952, says the fundamental model's specific returns still carry common variation the 17 columns do not span. E4 quantified it and handed the survivor-only cross-section to the data layer.

**The survivor-only cross-section is the binding data defect.** The sector dummies confine the model to the 502 names the sector file holds, a snapshot of current members, so the historical cross-section contains only companies in the index today. In 2010 the restriction removes 41.9 percent of the index by name and 8.85 percent by market capitalisation.

**No risk model version is calibrated, and the champion is declared with a stress haircut.** XS-v1 wins the champion rule with mean absolute bias deviation 0.0607, and the stored stress haircut of 1.8471 multiplies a stress forecast. The champion is provisional; the haircut is the honesty about that.

**The exact FMP hedge is perfect in-model and the ETF hedge is nearly so for the long-only book.** The exact hedge zeroes modeled exposure to 5.8e-15 at the cost of trading 461.9 names; the ETF hedge removes 0.978494 of factor variance with 13.03 instruments. For a momentum book the residual size and liquidity exposures survive, which is the cost of hedging with instruments rather than algebra.

**All six signals are NULL, and that is a complete result.** RG-Signal returned NULL for every signal. The construction machinery then ran on synthetic alpha with a known IC, which tests the machinery more cleanly than a real signal would, and the book runs as a documented null book with the expected verdict of luck written down before it started.

**The champion construction is the proportional rule with the exact FMP hedge.** Effective breadth is 128.705 against 456.63 names, and the transfer coefficient over effective breadth is what makes the proportional rule the champion; constrained mean-variance buys robustness at a transfer-coefficient cost.

**The cost of a high-turnover rebalance is impact, not spread.** The spread schedule says a raw reading of 4 percent overstates the cost by orders of magnitude; the real cost is about 0.07 percent of AUM. Capacity is impact-limited, and the lowest-IC book is cost-impaired at any size.

**Half Kelly, no drawdown stop, no volatility targeting.** The drawdown stop and volatility targeting each fail their pre-registered bar against the control, and the risk budget is written per VIX regime. Half Kelly is the sizing lever.

**The paper book is a dry run waiting on the owner.** NAV is 1,000,000, final. The thirty-day clock exists and the first static-day start was voided because the frozen close would produce thirty identical proposals. dry_run stays true until the owner flips it once. The construction choice waits on the owner, and the table that is the decision surface is built.

## Blockers and limitations

The cross-section is survivors only, the share count history starts late, the risk-free series ends before the price series, and the constituent source is frozen, all as recorded in the earlier sprints. E4 owns the survivor-only cross-section and the coverage gap. E5's answer is that no version is calibrated, so every risk number carries a stress haircut. The live loop still waits on three owner-held things: the construction choice, the dry-run flip, and the sign-off to re-run the universe reconstruction against the ongoing constituent source.

The ongoing constituent source exists and the daily archive has begun, but it is not yet applied. The SSGA SPY daily holdings file is fetched, validated by shape, filtered to 503 equity rows matching the live Wikipedia count, and archived one dated file per as-of date from 2026-09-18. It carries a CUSIP and a SEDOL per row, which is the security-identity source the project named. It provides no history, so the point-in-time record starts accumulating from today and reconstructs nothing before it. Re-running the reconstruction against it is a plan-changing event and gets its own task with the owner's sign-off.

The paper book runs on a documented null signal, so E12 will attribute luck rather than skill. That is the design: the machinery is what is being validated, not the signal.

## What the process itself taught

The early sprints taught that documentation is a verification step, not a summary of work already verified, and that a stored probe record catches the zero that arithmetic says is correct. The versioning discipline, the manifest hash and the revisions block, is what makes a correction auditable: the entry recording a correction carries the old value beside the new one, so a reader sees both the error and its size.

The later sprints taught three more things. First, the champion rule written before any number was seen is what makes E5's provisional champion honest: the model wins on the rule and loses on calibration, and the stress haircut is the recorded compromise. Second, a null signal result is a complete outcome, not a failed sprint: the RG-Signal exit routes construction to synthetic alpha, and the machinery is tested more cleanly for it. Third, the live loop's defects are found by running it dry: the static-day start that would produce thirty identical proposals was caught before the clock started, and the construction table's realized-beta column was decomposed before the owner read it, because a number that means more than it says is worse than no number.

The last rule is that no figure in a walkthrough may be typed by hand. Each sprint's walkthrough notebook reproduces the sprint's numbers from the artifacts, asserts each printed value against its stored counterpart, and ends with a cell that collects every stored value into a forbidden set. The same rule now applies to this report, enforced by a traceability test that fails the moment a quoted number and a stored measurement disagree.

## What comes next and what it is for

E11's remaining work is the owner's: pick a construction from the table, flip dry_run once to start the thirty-day clock, and sign off on the universe reconstruction against the ongoing constituent source. The dashboard is deployment-ready and renders the stored proposal with the construction named on screen.

E12 explains every dollar the paper book made or lost, decomposing profit into factor and idiosyncratic pieces from holdings, cross-checking against a returns-based regression, and separating skill from luck with Sharpe standard errors. Because the book runs on a documented null signal, the expected E12 verdict is luck, and that expectation is written down before the book starts.

E13 writes the credit port design, with no code. Most of the mathematics transfers unchanged: the weighted least squares fit, the factor-mimicking portfolios, the identification constraint, the covariance assembly, the risk decomposition, the hedging algebra and the holdings-based attribution are all linear algebra. What has to be re-specified is the data and the meaning of the columns. The regressand becomes an excess return over a duration-matched Treasury. The descriptors become spread duration, spread level, rating and sector dummies, issue size, age, liquidity and spread momentum. The specific risk term stops being a small residual and becomes a large, persistent, issuer-level quantity. The survivor restriction should shrink, because rating and sector files exist and sit closer to point-in-time than a snapshot of current index members, while the cross-section becomes far thinner per day, so the rank deficiency that appeared on none of the equity days becomes routine. The exposure timing problem is unchanged and arguably worse. That is the value of doing the equity version first: the mathematics is the same, the failure modes are the same, and the equity panel is large enough to find them before the data gets hard.

Numbers here are quoted as the project stores them; where a figure reads as rounded, it is rounded for speech and the stored value is longer.
