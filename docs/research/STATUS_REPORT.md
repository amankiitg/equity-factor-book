# Equity Factor Book: Status Report

## What this project is and why it exists

The Equity Factor Book builds every component of a systematic equity risk and portfolio management stack from scratch, using the two books by Giuseppe Paleologo as the syllabus. The goal is not a tradeable signal but the ability to build and defend each piece of the machinery, on free data, in a form that ports to corporate credit. The roadmap puts it plainly: build the machinery, not an edge. No signal here is claimed to have an edge, a profitable strategy is not required for the project to succeed, and a negative result counts as a complete outcome if it prevents a bad decision later.

Free data is a deliberate constraint: prices and share counts from a retail vendor, index membership from a pinned Wikipedia revision of the S&P 500 changes table, factor returns from the Kenneth French library, sectors from the Wikipedia constituents table. That forces the project to confront missing history, revised history, reused ticker symbols and non-point-in-time sector maps, which is where the interesting engineering lives.

Equities before credit, for three reasons. Equity prices are liquid and daily, so a factor model is testable on a clean panel before the same mathematics meets instruments that trade by appointment. Equity cross-sections are wide, five hundred names a day, which makes a daily cross-sectional regression well posed. And equities supply a large free panel of failures, the delisted and the acquired, which is what makes survivorship bias measurable rather than theoretical. Credit is the destination, but its data problems deserve to be met by someone who has met the easier version.

## The plan

The work is thirteen sprints in three tiers. Tier one, E1 to E3, is committed and is what this report covers: data, time-series models, the cross-sectional risk model. Tier two, E4 to E8, is the risk model and construction stack. Tier three, E9 to E13, covers costs and capacity, dynamic risk allocation, the paper-traded book, attribution, and the credit port design. Only one date is committed: gate G1 on September 20, 2026, which requires E1 to E3 running end to end.

Every sprint follows the same loop: a concept is stated, given an intuition, written in its academic formulation, implemented, tested empirically, interpreted the way a portfolio manager would use it, and written up as a research artifact. A concept that stops before the last two steps is a textbook exercise. Each sprint opens with three research questions, one academic, one practitioner and one research, and ends with a document for a reader who has not seen the code.

Every sprint also writes down, before the numbers exist, what would falsify it. A pre-registered falsification criterion is a numeric threshold stated in advance, stored in the sprint's plan, and evaluated afterwards without being reworded. E2 pre-registered that two volatility estimators would beat a trailing 252-day estimate for more than sixty percent of names. E3 pre-registered that its average cross-sectional R squared would exceed twenty percent, with anything below fifteen percent read as evidence that the descriptor construction was wrong rather than that the market was unusual. Writing the threshold first stops a researcher who sees a disappointing number from inventing a reason the number was the right one. Pre-registration turns that discretion into a recorded verdict, and no criterion is ever narrowed after the fact.

Three research gates sit in front of the major engineering efforts. RG-Data at the end of E1 asks whether every source returned rows, whether coverage cleared its threshold, and whether the survivorship magnitude is small enough for long and short research; a negative answer means adapting the universe, recording the decision and re-running. RG-Signal at the end of E7 asks whether a signal survives out-of-sample testing and a multiple-testing hurdle; a negative answer labels the signal null, and if no signal passes, the construction stack is still built and tested on synthetic alpha with a known information coefficient, so a null result about markets still teaches the machinery. RG-Operate at the end of E10 asks whether a champion risk model exists with a stated stress haircut, and whether the hedge policy, the cost model and the risk budget are written down; if it fails, the paper-traded book waits, because running a book on an unvalidated risk model teaches operations and nothing else.

## What has actually been built

**Sprint E1, the data layer.** The academic question was what a return is and which definition suits which operation, the practitioner question was whether the prices, universe and risk-free rate can be trusted, and the research question was how large the survivorship bias is. The sprint built a price and returns pipeline with explicit missing-data, delisting, staleness and outlier policies; a point-in-time universe rebuilt by walking the index changes table backwards; a performance library with Sharpe standard errors; dashboard tab D0 for data health; and an append-only ledger recording every data decision with its reason. Four of five criteria failed. Coverage of current index members was complete, but coverage of all requested tickers was 77 percent and of tickers with ten years of history 71 percent, against a 95 percent bar. The returns file has 302 interior missing return days across 13 tickers, so the zero-NaN criterion failed. The adjusted-close audit averaged 0.018 basis points against a close-plus-dividend series but peaked at 221 basis points on one merger day, so the maximum criterion failed. The equal-weight universe return correlates with the French market return at 0.9564. The survivorship measurement failed its threshold in the most informative way: 44.8 percent of deleted members have recoverable price history against a 70 percent bar, the naive backtest that buys all current members earns 365.10 basis points per year more than a point-in-time universe, and that universe earns 8.24 basis points per year more than the French market. The rule a portfolio manager takes from E1: every long-only result here carries a survivorship caveat.

**Sprint E2, time-series models and volatility.** The questions were how a stock's factor exposure is estimated and how uncertain that estimate is, how much a beta of 1.3 should be believed, and whether shrunk or adaptive estimators beat raw ones out of sample. The sprint built a model version registered as TS-v1: market and factor betas with classical and Newey-West standard errors, Vasicek and Blume shrinkage, rolling and exponentially weighted betas, several volatility estimators including GARCH, Fama-French five-factor plus momentum residuals, and a portfolio risk history for two seed books, on dashboard tab D1. The registry was introduced here: a model version with its parameters and the hash of the data it was fitted on, and a champion rule written before any number was seen. TS-v1 is not champion and not eligible, because the rule requires a champion to be refreshable daily from the project's own data. Nine of thirteen criteria passed, among them full-sample betas agreeing with rolling ones at 0.9244, a mean pairwise residual correlation of 0.0156, and an equal-weight book bias statistic averaging 1.0225, running from 0.68 in 2012 to 1.39 in 2020. Four failed, three in the volatility horse race: GARCH beat trailing volatility for 58.2 percent of the 98 names it could fit and the decay 0.94 estimator for only 35.4 percent, against a sixty percent bar, and neither cleared the bar at a 21-day horizon either, so production stayed with a decay 0.97 estimate. What a portfolio manager takes from E2: use a shrunk rolling beta with a Newey-West standard error for hedging, size positions off idiosyncratic volatility rather than total volatility, and treat an eyeballed Sharpe ratio, which here carries a standard error near 0.23, as a hypothesis rather than evidence.

**Sprint E3, the cross-sectional model.** The questions were how cross-sectional returns decompose into systematic and idiosyncratic pieces and whether the premia are distinguishable from zero, how much portfolio risk comes from unintended factor exposures and which position reduces risk fastest if trimmed, and whether the model explains realized risk well enough to size a book. A factor model writes each stock's return as its exposures to a handful of common factors times those factors' returns, with the leftover called idiosyncratic risk. The sprint built a fundamental cross-sectional model registered as XS-v1: seven style descriptors, market capitalisation, size, beta, momentum, reversal, residual volatility and liquidity, all dated one day before the return they explain; eleven sector factors; a weighted least squares fit of 17 estimated columns per day with the market factor as the intercept; factor-mimicking portfolios; a factor covariance matrix and a shrunk specific variance; a risk decomposition for both seed books; Fama-MacBeth premia with Newey-West t statistics; an exposure time series and realized residual covariances; dashboard tab D2 with eight panels; and three artifacts added while writing the close-out, bias by exposure tercile, bias beside model coverage, and the survivor restriction. XS-v1 runs from January 2011, the first session on which at least 300 names carry every descriptor, over 3,941 sessions to September 2026, holding between 428 and 497 names a day. Eight of nine criteria passed. The average cross-sectional R squared is 0.3295. A factor-mimicking portfolio is one built from the cross-section with unit exposure to one factor and zero to every other; XS-v1's identity for these portfolios holds to 1.2 times ten to the minus thirteen, and its identification constraint to 7.0 times ten to the minus eighteen. Its momentum factor correlates with the French momentum factor at 0.756 and its market factor with the French total market return at 0.9889. The decomposition identity holds to machine precision and the contribution sum matches portfolio volatility to 6.2 times ten to the minus seventeen, so each position's marginal contribution to risk, the change in volatility per unit of weight, sums exactly to total volatility. The Fama-MacBeth table, which averages the daily cross-sectional factor returns through time and t-tests their mean to ask whether a factor was paid for, covers 18 factors over four periods with 72 premia, 64 of them labelled unpriced and kept rather than dropped. The failure is the bias statistic, which is realized volatility divided by predicted volatility over a window: the equal-weight book's mean monthly bias is 0.9570, inside the 0.8 to 1.25 band, while the momentum book's is 0.6664, below it, with 12.1 percent of its months in band against 36.9 percent for the equal-weight book. What a portfolio manager takes from E3: the model explains about a third of daily cross-sectional dispersion, its factor structure is internally exact, it prices the equal-weight book acceptably on average, and it is not yet fit to size the momentum book.

Across the three sprints the repository holds 45 hashed artifacts under a single manifest hash, 335 passing tests, and a dashboard with four tabs. The registry holds two model versions, neither champion.

## The findings

**Survivorship bias got worse, not better, after the data was cleaned.** Survivorship bias is the distortion that appears when a historical dataset contains only the companies that survived to the present, so the losers are missing and the average looks better than it was. E1 measured that only 44.8 percent of the index's 355 deleted members have recoverable price history, and that the naive backtest of today's members earns 365.10 basis points per year more than a point-in-time universe. The revision moved the wrong way: the first measurement was 349.67 basis points per year on a panel of 858 tickers, correcting ticker identity took the panel to 822 tickers and the bias to 365.81, and a second restatement settled it at 365.10. The bias grew because the names the identity check removed had been diluting the survivor advantage. Had that work been skipped, every long-only result would carry a survivorship number about five percent too small.

**A ticker symbol is not an identity.** This began with one implausible day: a ticker whose adjusted close jumped from 0.196 to 18.90 on 2026-05-18, a 9,542.9 percent return with no split to explain it. The hygiene layer had flagged the row, but the volatility code was reading raw returns, so that single row moved the trailing baseline's mean loss from minus 6.61 to minus 1.82. The jump turned out to be a reused symbol, a 2026 listing that had taken the ticker of a 2010 to 2011 index member, with the vendor splicing two companies into one series. Three more names matched the pattern, and the general check found 36 reused symbols among the 373 tickers on the index changes table's removed list. Thirty-three left the estimation panel and three, renames rather than takeovers, were restored with truncated history, so coverage of current constituents is now 502 of 503. Unnoticed, every result before 2017 would have carried another company's returns, and because failures are the names most likely to be reused as tickers, the contamination would have sat where a long-short book does its damage.

**The simplest volatility estimator was not beaten, and the project accepted that.** The pre-registered claim was that GARCH and a decay 0.94 estimator would each beat a trailing 252-day volatility estimate for more than sixty percent of names, on a loss function called QLIKE, which scores a forecast as the log of the forecast variance plus realized variance divided by the forecast variance, lower better. Both failed. The project ruled out the explanations that would have made it an artefact: forecast and target confirmed one step ahead on both sides, and 98 of 100 GARCH fits converging. Two measurements then reframed it. Counting names, the decay 0.94 estimator wins for 35.4 percent; counting name-days, it wins on 51.6 percent of 242,126 observations, because a per-name average is dominated by a few names where one day is badly mispriced and the loss is unbounded. And it depends on the window: the trailing estimate beats the decay 0.97 estimator for 52.0 percent of names over two years, while that estimator beats trailing for 86.8 percent of names over sixteen. The criterion is written on the out-of-sample window, so it fails and stays failed. Had the project taken the first framing, it would have replaced an estimator well calibrated over sixteen years on the strength of a two-year horse race.

**A book that re-sorts every month has an exposure that static betas cannot see.** The exposure timing problem is that a portfolio rebuilt on a signal every month holds a large factor exposure whenever it is measured at the moment it is held, but a full-sample regression of its returns on the factor averages that exposure toward zero. The momentum book's static decomposition put its factor share of variance at 0.0968, which reads as an almost entirely idiosyncratic book. Measuring it with the weights actually held and 252-day betas dated at each rebalance put the factor share at 0.7390 over 195 rebalances, with the momentum exposure averaging 0.0698, while the static aggregate with the last month's weights reported minus 0.0162. Its return series loads 0.2885 on the momentum factor with a Newey-West t statistic of 29.10. Its bias statistic averaged 1.8544 by year against 1.0225 for the equal-weight book, so the model understated its risk by nearly a factor of two. No risk number for a long-short book may be quoted without the measurement method attached.

**The E3 tercile diagnostic located the momentum book's error in the factor covariance.** E3 found the same book failing its bias criterion in the opposite direction, at 0.6664, meaning the model over-predicted risk by roughly one and a half times where E2 had pointed the other way. Since the two numbers measure different constructions, the sprint split the book's 141 months into terciles of its own momentum exposure: bias is 0.8960 in the lowest, 0.5841 in the middle, 0.5191 in the highest, so the over-prediction concentrates where the exposure is largest. Across those terciles predicted volatility barely moves, from 0.0547 to 0.0582, a swing of 0.0037, while realized volatility falls from 0.0479 to 0.0292, a swing of 0.0187. The factor term, the only term that responds to the book's own exposure, does not respond. The specific variance errs the other way, moving the factor share from 0.7906 on a diagonal model to 0.5890 once residual co-movement is admitted. The equal-weight book, which has no momentum tilt, is flat across the same buckets at 0.9413, 0.9502 and 0.9795. Had the sprint stopped with the two bias numbers, the next move would have been to tune the specific variance, and the factor covariance would have been left alone.

## Blockers and limitations

The cross-section is survivors only. The sector dummies confine the model to the 502 names the sector file holds, and that file is a snapshot of current index members, so the historical cross-section contains only companies in the index today. In 2010 the restriction removes 41.9 percent of the index by name and 8.85 percent by market capitalisation, falling to 4.8 percent by name and 0.24 percent by cap in 2025. Look-ahead bias is using a number in a historical test that was not knowable on the date it applies to, and that is what this is. It is not fixable within the current data, E4 owns it, and its cost is that the premia, the R squared and the specific risk estimates are all flattered by the absence of failures.

The share count history starts late. The vendor's share counts are as filed rather than restated, so they are usable, but almost nothing was filed before late 2015: one name has a count in April 2013, 22 by September 2015, 396 by November 2015, and all 502 sector-mapped names by 2017. 1,245,448 name-days, 36 percent of the panel, carry a count backfilled from a name's first filing, each flagged. Because market capitalisation is price times shares, and the estimator weights observations by the square root of market capitalisation, the look-ahead reaches both the Size descriptor and the regression weights: before October 2015, 99.8 percent of name-days carry the flag and the flagged names hold essentially all the weight, against a flagged weight of 0.05 percent in 2026. The project has not measured what re-estimating from October 2015 alone would do, because that run has not been made; it is the sprint's recommendation for E4. Fixable only by paying for a point-in-time share count source.

The risk-free series ends before the price series, so XS-v1 regresses total returns rather than excess returns, which makes its market factor a total-return factor and its premia total-return premia. The French series ends 2026-07-31 while prices run to 2026-09-03, and the cost was measured: fitting both ways on the 3,917 overlapping days gives a correlation of 0.9999726435 between the two market factors, the difference being the risk-free rate itself, since the market factor is the intercept. Not fixable without a longer risk-free series, owned by whoever extends the data layer, cost nil in practice.

The equal-weight book is only partly covered, because it is equal weighted across the whole panel while the model universe is the 502 sector-mapped names. The weight held in names with no descriptor row runs from 0.4172 in 2011 to 0.0179 in 2026, so the bias statistic measures the part of the book the model can see. Same dependency as the survivor item, owned by E4, and the cost is completeness rather than correctness.

The momentum book fails its bias criterion, at 0.6664 against an 0.8 floor, with 12.1 percent of months in band. This is the sprint's one recorded failure and it is not softened: the model cannot yet size that book. E4 owns the fix, a covariance conditioned on the book's own exposure, and E3's tercile table is the acceptance test, because a fix that does not flatten the bias across the exposure buckets has not addressed the measured cause.

The constituent source is frozen. Membership comes from a pinned Wikipedia revision dated 2026-08-10, because the live page stopped publishing the changes table the next day, so the universe is correct to 2026-08-05 and static after. A paper-traded book cannot run on a frozen universe, so E11 must secure an ongoing constituent source first.

## What the process itself taught

Three sprints running, the defect of the sprint was found by writing the explanation rather than by a test. In E2 it was the dashboard's rolling beta panel, which looked for a column structure the build never writes and drew an empty chart while every neighbouring panel worked; the walkthrough section that maps each panel to the columns it reads made the mismatch visible. In E2 again it was the GARCH evaluation sample, which was the alphabetical head of a sorted ticker list, meaning names starting with A and B. Writing the close-out made the arbitrariness obvious, and on a seeded random sample of 100 names the same estimator's measured win share moved from 46.7 to 61.2 percent at one horizon and from 55.6 to 49.0 percent at the other, on the same data. In E3 it was two things at once: the exposure time series, which concatenated two books without a label, so a stored criterion had been quoting a blend of two exposure vectors, and the survivor restriction table, whose market capitalisation column came back as a clean zero because the build had narrowed a cached share history to the sector file and no outside name had a market capitalisation at all. That second one is the sharpest lesson: the zero was arithmetically correct, nothing raised, and only the stored probe record caught it. Documentation here is a verification step, not a summary of work already verified.

The versioning discipline follows from the same reasoning. Nothing is overwritten. Every artifact is hashed into a manifest with one combined hash, each model version carries the hash of the data it was fitted on, and each sprint's results file carries its criteria, their stored numbers, and a revisions block reporting which numbers moved between builds. When the panel was corrected, E1's results file gained a history entry per data hash, and the project kept the entry for an intermediate build that changed nothing, because the chain of hashes is the record. When a correction is made, the entry recording it carries the old value beside the new one, so a reader sees both the error and its size: 349.67 basis points becoming 365.10, a factor share of 0.0939 becoming 0.7390, a window of 47 months becoming 141.

The last rule is that no figure in a walkthrough may be typed by hand. Each sprint's walkthrough notebook reproduces the sprint's numbers from the artifacts, asserts each printed value against its stored counterpart, and ends with a cell that collects every stored value into a forbidden set and asserts none appears as a literal anywhere in the notebook's code. In E2 that set held 212 values and 135 numeric checks ran inside the notebook; E3 collects 571 values and carries 84 assertions, up from 40 before the close-out sections were added. A notebook that reads its numbers cannot go stale.

## What comes next and what it is for

E4 builds statistical factor models and a covariance estimator laboratory. It fits principal components to the returns and, separately, to the specific returns XS-v1 leaves behind, which tests whether a common factor is missing from the fundamental model; it compares sample, exponentially weighted, shrunk and eigenvalue-clipped covariance estimates on an out-of-sample minimum-variance portfolio; and it asks whether the largest eigenvalue of the residual covariance sits above the Marchenko-Pastur noise boundary. E4 also owns the survivor-only cross-section and the coverage gap, because both need the same point-in-time constituent and sector source. E5 evaluates the risk models the way a vendor model would be, with bias statistics, coverage tests and horizon consistency across families of portfolios and across regimes, and it declares a champion by the rule written down in E2: the smallest mean absolute deviation of the bias statistic from one, ties broken toward fewer parameters, and a champion refreshable daily from the project's own data. E6 is hedging: how much unwanted beta and factor exposure can be removed with instruments that can actually be traded, and what the hedge costs in turnover, borrow and basis risk. E7 is the signal laboratory, where information coefficients, decay and factor-neutral quantile returns are computed and every variant logged. E8 turns expected returns and the champion covariance into positions, comparing simple sizing rules against constrained mean-variance with alpha noise.

The remaining sprints, a sentence each. E9 makes construction cost-aware with a turnover-penalised optimizer and a capacity curve. E10 decides how much risk to run given an estimated Sharpe ratio, and tests whether volatility targeting or a stop-loss improves the seed book against an independent random control. E11 assembles everything into a daily long-short paper book that runs forward for thirty trading days, with an evening proposal, morning execution and daily reconciliation of forecast to outcome. E12 explains every dollar the book made or lost, decomposing profit into factor and idiosyncratic pieces from holdings, cross-checking against a returns-based regression, and separating skill from luck with Sharpe standard errors. E13 writes the credit port design, with no code.

The credit port is what makes the exercise worth doing. Most of the mathematics transfers unchanged: a weighted least squares cross-sectional fit, the factor-mimicking portfolios, the identification constraint that makes group factor returns sum to zero, the covariance assembly, the risk decomposition, the hedging algebra and the holdings-based attribution are all linear algebra. What has to be re-specified is the data and the meaning of the columns. The regressand becomes an excess return over a duration-matched Treasury, because a credit return has to be separated from the rate move that dominates it. The descriptors become spread duration or its dollar equivalent, spread level, rating and sector dummies, issue size, age, liquidity and spread momentum. The specific risk term stops being a small residual and becomes a large, persistent, issuer-level quantity, because bonds of one issuer are not independent and a diagonal assumption will be wrong there in a way it mostly is not in equities. Two of this project's problems change sign. The survivor restriction should shrink, because rating and sector files exist and sit closer to point-in-time than a snapshot of current index members, while the cross-section becomes far thinner per day, so the rank deficiency that appeared on none of 3,941 equity days becomes routine. The exposure timing problem is unchanged and arguably worse: a book sorted on spread carries a dynamic exposure to the spread factor, so an unconditional covariance will fail to price it for exactly the reason the momentum book failed here, and the fix is the conditional covariance E4 owes this project now. That is the value of doing the equity version first: the mathematics is the same, the failure modes are the same, and the equity panel is large enough to find them before the data gets hard.

Numbers here are quoted as the project stores them; where a figure reads as rounded, it is rounded for speech and the stored value is longer.
